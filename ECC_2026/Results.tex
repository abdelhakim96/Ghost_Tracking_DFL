The level flight trajectory serves as a baseline validation of the controller's tracking performance under quasi-steady conditions. The fixed-wing aircraft is trimmed for cruise and held by its outer attitude-hold loop, exposing the controller to a near-constant forward-velocity reference. The position MAE settles at $0.26$ m (Table~\ref{tab:mae_summary}), the camera-orientation MAE remains at the numerical-precision floor of $2\times 10^{-4}\,^\circ$, and the multicopter body pitches forward to generate the forward thrust required to match the fixed-wing's speed while the gimbal actively counter-rotates so that the camera orientation precisely follows the fixed-wing attitude. This behaviour directly demonstrates the pose matching strategy presented in Section~\ref{sec:method}, where the dynamic feedback control $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_\text{MC}$ is computed via Eq.~\eqref{eq:feedback_control_extended} to satisfy $\mathbf{q}_\text{M} \otimes \mathbf{q}_\text{G} = \mathbf{q}_\text{A}$. The rapid gimbal response and the absence of steady-state error confirm that the system operates well inside the reachable region defined by Assumption~\ref{ass:non_singularity}, maintaining full actuation authority throughout the trajectory.

To stress-test the DFL controller under highly dynamic conditions, we command the fixed-wing aircraft to perform a vertical loop with a sustained elevator deflection. This manoeuvre generates rapid variations in position, velocity, and orientation, with the aircraft experiencing significant changes in airspeed and load factor throughout the loop. As evident in Fig.~\ref{fig:3d_trajectory_loop}, the multicopter--gimbal system successfully completes the loop, with the camera trajectory closely following the fixed-wing path. Fig.~\ref{fig:loop_tracking} confirms that the camera Euler angles match the fixed-wing reference even as the trajectory passes through inverted flight, where the pitch saturates at $\pm 90^\circ$ and the roll/yaw conventions flip by $180^\circ$. The position MAE stays at $0.26$ m and the orientation MAE at $2\times 10^{-4}\,^\circ$. The brief transients during the highest-acceleration phases validate the feedback linearization approach developed in Section~\ref{sec:method}, where the dynamic compensator~\eqref{eq:new_quad_dynamics} extends the relative degree to enable exact input-output decoupling. The linearized dynamics allow the controller to compute the required thrust derivatives $\ddot{T}$ and torques $\boldsymbol{\tau}_\text{M}$ that drive the tracking errors toward zero.

The remaining manoeuvres exercise the controller against the full aerobatic catalogue: a barrel roll, an Immelmann turn, a Split-S, and a Cuban-eight. The complete numerical summary is given in Table~\ref{tab:mae_summary}, and the time-resolved camera-orientation error for all four cases is plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:aerobatic_err}. Across the catalogue, the orientation geodesic error stays at the integrator-tolerance floor ($\approx 10^{-4}\,^\circ$), with brief excursions of at most $0.13^\circ$ at the highest-load reversals of the Split-S. The position MAE grows with manoeuvre duration and complexity, from $0.31$ m on the $2.5$ s barrel roll to $1.37$ m on the Split-S, but never exceeds a small fraction of the manoeuvre length. These results confirm that the same dynamic feedback law generalises to the entire aerobatic envelope without per-manoeuvre re-tuning.

\subsection{Discussion}
% The simulation results validate the theoretical framework presented in Sections~\ref{sec:proof} and \ref{sec:method}. The multicopter-gimbal system successfully achieves camera-pose mimicry $\mathbf{y}_\text{MC}(t) \equiv \mathbf{y}_\text{AC}(t)$ for both steady and aggressive trajectories, confirming output full actuation on $SE(3)$, effective attitude decoupling, and the efficacy of the DFL controller with dynamic compensation in achieving precise tracking of the nonlinear, coupled multicopter-gimbal dynamics. The high-amplitude pitch oscillations observed in the multicopter body orientation during both trajectories, especially pronounced in the loop maneuver, highlight the inherent challenge of fixed-wing mimicry, where the multicopter must continuously reorient its thrust vector to follow the aircraft’s velocity profile. The gimbal’s ability to maintain smooth camera orientation despite these aggressive motions demonstrates the practical value of the proposed architecture.
The simulation results validate the theoretical framework presented in Sections~\ref{sec:proof} and~\ref{sec:method}. The multicopter--gimbal system successfully achieves camera-pose mimicry $\mathbf{y}_\text{MC}(t) \equiv \mathbf{y}_\text{AC}(t)$ for both steady and aggressive trajectories, confirming output full actuation on $SE(3)$, effective attitude decoupling, and the efficacy of the DFL controller with dynamic compensation in achieving precise tracking of the nonlinear, coupled multicopter--gimbal dynamics. The high-amplitude pitch oscillations observed in the multicopter body orientation during the loop, barrel roll, Immelmann, Split-S, and Cuban-eight highlight the inherent challenge of fixed-wing mimicry, where the multicopter must continuously reorient its thrust vector to follow the aircraft's velocity profile. The gimbal's ability to maintain a smooth camera orientation despite these aggressive motions demonstrates the practical value of the proposed architecture, and the orientation error remaining at the numerical-precision floor across full $360^\circ$ rotations matches the global-controllability prediction associated with the 3-axis gimbal extension noted after Eq.~\eqref{eq:S_determinant}.
