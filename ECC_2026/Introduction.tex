% % Motivation
% %Mimicking one dynamic system with another can enable low-cost, safe, and repeatable testing and data collection, algorithm validation, operational flexibility across environments, and output equivalence for perception-driven tasks without the logistical or risk of the target platform.

% The increasing complexity and cost of controlling specific dynamical systems, such as fixed-wing aircraft, pose significant challenges in training, simulation and real-world operations. Fixed-wing platforms are constrained by aerodynamic principles that limit their manoeuvrability. As they cannot hover or fly sideways, they must maintain a minimum airspeed, and their stability margins depend significantly on flight conditions ~\cite{morando2025trajectory}. These constraints make fixed-wing systems demanding in both operation and replication in controlled environments~\cite{li2024high}. By contrast, quadcopters are mechanically simpler, more affordable, and much more maneuverable. They can hover, fly laterally, and execute aggressive manoeuvres within confined spaces, making them attractive candidates for mimicking the behaviour of a more complex aerial system. If a quadcopter emulated the dynamics of a fixed-wing aircraft, it would open up practical applications in low-cost training, simulation and autonomy research. %For example, a quadcopter equipped with a gimbal-mounted camera could reproduce fixed-wing trajectories for pilot training or algorithm validation without requiring access to a real fixed-wing aircraft. 

% % Challenges
% Despite this potential, emulating one aerial system with another presents several significant challenges. The dynamics of fixed-wing aircraft and quadcopter vehicles differ fundamentally; one relies on continuous forward motion to generate lift, while the other produces lift through vertical thrust. Nonholonomic constraints, aerodynamic stall behaviour, differences in actuation and stability characteristics all make direct mapping between the two systems complex. A practical solution must therefore include a rigorous mechanism that can analyse the similarities between the two systems, derive mappings between their states and inputs, and ensure that the secondary system (multicopter) accurately mimics the primary system (fixed-wing aircraft) under realistic flight conditions ~\cite{yuan2025design, wu2019modeling}.

Reproducing the behaviour of one dynamical system with another, here called emulation, supports training, algorithm validation, and data collection. Software simulators reproduce a system and its sensory environment in a virtual world~\cite{shah2017airsim, amer2023unav}, but models tuned on synthetic data lose accuracy when transferred to hardware, the so-called sim-to-real gap~\cite{gao2020realitygap, aljalbout2025reality}. Pilot-in-the-loop simulators add a physical motion platform for human training~\cite{stroosma2003using, edvani1997simona, vanpaassen2006simona}, yet their sensory stream is rendered and does not close the loop around the controlled system. This paper considers a third setting, in which one physical system reproduces, in real time, the motion and the camera viewpoint of a second physical system.

Concretely, a multicopter carrying a camera on a gimbal is required to reproduce the camera pose, that is, the position and orientation, of a fixed-wing aircraft. The fixed-wing is constrained by a minimum airspeed and by nonholonomic motion, whereas the multicopter can hover, translate laterally, and fly aggressive manoeuvres, which makes it an inexpensive surrogate for testing perception and control algorithms in real environments~\cite{smith2022emulation}. The two platforms are dynamically dissimilar: the fixed-wing produces lift through forward flight and has no static counterpart to the multicopter thrust vectoring~\cite{yuan2025design, wu2019modeling}. Emulation is therefore a nonlinear output-matching problem on $SE(3)$ between two underactuated systems, and it raises two questions: whether exact matching is achievable, and by which control law.

Feasibility has been studied through similarity measures, which map the trajectories of one system to those of another and score the mismatch by a cost functional~\cite{wang2023similarity}, and through lifting techniques, which embed dissimilar dynamics in a common linear space~\cite{xiao2021lifting, quan2025lifting}. For the control law, model predictive control enforces input constraints explicitly~\cite{amer2023visual, amer2025, alexis2016mpc} at a cost that is high for the dimension and bandwidth required here. Exact feedback linearization is an alternative~\cite{isidori1995nonlinear, isidori1981nonlinear}: a regular state feedback transforms the input-output map into a chain of integrators, after which tracking reduces to a linear design. The camera-pose output is not linearizable by static feedback, since the thrust acts only on the translational channel and the decoupling matrix is singular; extending the input with an integrator on the thrust restores a well-defined vector relative degree~\cite{Isidori1986CDC, Mistler2001ROMAN} and yields dynamic feedback linearization. The construction is related to differential flatness~\cite{fliess1995flatness}, used for aggressive quadrotor tracking~\cite{mellinger2011minimum, faessler2018differential, lee2010geometric}, and to nonlinear output regulation~\cite{byrnes1990output}.

Two points are usually left open. First, exact linearization is derived without actuator limits, so the synthesized inputs need not be realizable; this is commonly settled by assuming sufficient control authority, which cannot be verified a priori. Second, a two-axis gimbal carries a roll singularity that obstructs full-attitude reproduction. We treat both. The decoupling matrix is invertible by construction, so the actuator and gimbal limits are transported through the inverse to obtain a constructive description of the references a given platform can reproduce, and a third gimbal axis removes the roll singularity.

The contributions are the following. First, we cast physical-to-physical emulation as camera-pose mimicry on $SE(3)$ and show that a multicopter with a three-axis gimbal is output fully actuated and small-time locally controllable for the camera pose. Second, we obtain an exact dynamic-feedback-linearizing law for the mimicry problem and realize it with a single-parameter critically damped gain assignment and a singularity-robust inverse. Third, we characterize the feasible reference set as the preimage of the input-constraint set under the system inverse, and we add a reference governor that confines any reference to this set, turning the customary authority assumption into a checkable condition. Fourth, we validate the framework in simulation over a range of aerobatic manoeuvres. An open-source implementation accompanies the paper.\footnote{Repository: \url{https://github.com/abdelhakim96/Ghost_Tracking_DFL}}
